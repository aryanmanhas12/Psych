Global Frameworks

A local tool, built against global commitments.

Every government at the World Health Assembly has already agreed what good mental health care looks like. The gap is not consensus — it is delivery. This page sets out the international commitments this project was designed against, the evidence behind each, and precisely which of them a free screening tool can and cannot advance.

Part I — The Global Picture

What the world's own data says

These are WHO's published figures, not this project's estimates. They describe a system-level failure of delivery that is remarkably consistent across income levels — and worst where resources are scarcest.

1 billion+people worldwide are living with a mental health conditionWHO, September 2025
2%median share of government health budgets spent on mental health — unchanged since 2017WHO Mental Health Atlas 2024
13mental health workers per 100,000 people — the global medianWHO Mental Health Atlas 2024
727,000lives lost to suicide in 2021; the third leading cause of death among 15–29-year-oldsWHO, 2025

The trajectory matters as much as the totals. On present trends the world is projected to achieve roughly a 12% reduction in suicide mortality by 2030, against a UN target of one third. That is not a marginal shortfall; it is a miss by a factor of nearly three, and it is the clearest single argument for interventions that work upstream of specialist services.

Part II — The Commitments

Four frameworks this was built against

These are not aspirations invented for this project. They are instruments adopted by member states, including India, and they define what "good" means in this field.

United Nations · Agenda 2030

Sustainable Development Goal target 3.4

SDG 3.4 commits every member state, by 2030, to "reduce by one third premature mortality from non-communicable diseases through prevention and treatment, and promote mental health and well-being." Indicator 3.4.2 tracks it by suicide mortality rate.

Mental health is therefore not a soft add-on to the development agenda. It is a numbered, measured target with a deadline four years away.

World Health Organization · Adopted by the World Health Assembly

Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2030

The plan sets four objectives for member states: stronger leadership and governance; comprehensive, integrated services in community-based settings; promotion and prevention strategies; and strengthened information systems, evidence and research. Each carries measurable global targets.

The second and third objectives are where a public screening tool has real standing: community-based access, and prevention through earlier identification.

World Health Organization · Implementation Programme

mhGAP — the Mental Health Gap Action Programme

mhGAP exists to scale up care for priority mental, neurological and substance use conditions by equipping non-specialists in primary and community settings to deliver evidence-based interventions — the approach usually called task-sharing.

The programme's central premise is that the treatment gap cannot be closed by producing specialists alone. This project is built on the same premise, one step earlier in the pathway: identification before referral.

World Health Organization · Rights and Quality

QualityRights

QualityRights works to improve service quality and to advance the rights of people with psychosocial, intellectual and cognitive disabilities, supporting systems built on recovery, rights and inclusion, and on reducing coercion, stigma and discrimination.

This is the framework that governs how a tool should behave, not just what it measures — and it is the reason this project publishes an ethics charter stating what it refuses to do.

Part III — Alignment, Stated Honestly

What this tool does — and what it cannot

Claiming to advance a WHO objective is easy. The table below states the specific mechanism in each case, and marks plainly where a screening tool contributes nothing. Overclaiming here would undermine the project's only real asset, which is that its claims can be checked.

Community-based access to careWHO Action Plan, Objective 2 Validated screening delivered free on any phone, offline-capable, in six languages, with no account, cost or clinic visit required to begin. Direct

Two of seven commitments this project cannot touch, and one it deliberately forgoes. A tool that claimed all seven would be worth less, not more.

Part IV — India, Where This Is Grounded

Global commitments have a national address

International frameworks only matter where they are implemented. India has an unusually complete legal and programmatic architecture for mental health — the shortfall is in reach, not in intent. This project is built to feed that architecture rather than duplicate it.

Statute

The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017

The Act establishes a legal right to access affordable, good-quality mental healthcare from services run or funded by the government, and aligns Indian law with the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

This matters for the tool's copy: every result that tells someone to seek care is pointing them toward something they are legally entitled to, not asking them to request a favour. The wording throughout reflects that.

Delivery

The District Mental Health Programme

The DMHP extends the National Mental Health Programme into districts, placing psychiatric care, free or near-free medication and counselling at the district hospital and below — the practical embodiment of WHO's community-based-services objective.

Every moderate and severe result in this tool routes explicitly to a DMHP clinic or PHC, because that is where a person can actually be seen. Naming the real service is worth more than generic advice to "seek help".

Access

Tele-MANAS

India's national tele-mental-health service offers free, 24×7, confidential support in 20+ languages, with escalation to specialists and linkage to district services — a direct instrument of the digital-access strand of global mental health policy.

Tele-MANAS appears on every page of this project and at every step of the guided conversation, because for most users it is a better first action than anything the software itself can offer.

Why the emphasis stays here

Specific beats generic

A screening tool that names PHQ-9 cutoffs but tells an Indian user only to "consult a professional" has done half the job. Naming Tele-MANAS, the DMHP clinic, the PHC and the legal right under the 2017 Act converts a score into an action someone can take this week.

The instruments are international; the pathway must be local. The architecture supports adding other countries' pathways the same way — the helpline layer is already worldwide and directory-first — but depth for one country was chosen over shallow coverage of many.
