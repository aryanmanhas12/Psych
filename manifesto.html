The Waiting
Room is Full.

In India, one psychiatrist serves a population the size of a small city. The result is three-minute consultations, missed diagnoses, and care reduced to a prescription refill. This is a manifesto for changing that.

Each grey dot ≈ 1,000 people. The gold dot is their one psychiatrist. India has roughly 0.75 psychiatrists per 100,000 people — the WHO recommends at least 3. Tap the gold dot to see the day's arithmetic.

The arithmetic: 100+ patients per OPD day ÷ 8 working hours ≈ under 5 minutes per person — history, diagnosis, prescription, counselling, all of it.

When one doctor sees a hundred patients before lunch, "negligence" stops being a personal failing and becomes a structural certainty. Diagnoses get compressed into checklists. Psychotherapy — the actual treatment for much of psychiatry — becomes a luxury no public hospital can afford to offer. Follow-ups collapse into refills.

The patient is blamed for not coming back. The doctor is blamed for not listening. The system that made both inevitable is blamed by no one.

Part II — The Manifesto

Five declarations

I

Time is a treatment

A psychiatric consultation compressed below the minimum time needed for a mental state examination is not care — it is triage pretending to be care. Minimum consultation time must be treated as a quality metric, audited like infection rates.

II

The psychiatrist cannot work alone

No country has ever closed a mental-health treatment gap by producing psychiatrists alone. Psychologists, psychiatric social workers, nurses, and trained community health workers are not assistants to the system — they are the system.

III

Primary care is the front line

Most depression, anxiety, and substance use should be identified and initially managed by trained general physicians at PHCs — as the District Mental Health Programme intended — reserving specialists for complexity, not volume.

IV

Distance is no longer an excuse

Tele-MANAS and telepsychiatry can put a specialist's judgement into every district hospital and every panchayat. A phone line staffed well beats a clinic that exists only on paper.

V

Rights on paper must become rights in practice

The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 promises every Indian the right to affordable, quality mental healthcare. A right that cannot be exercised in the average district is not a right — it is a press release. Budgets must follow the law.

Part III — The Way Out

Solutions that scale

Policy · Central & State

Task-sharing, formalized

Train and certify non-specialist health workers (ASHAs, PHC nurses, counsellors) to screen, deliver brief psychological interventions, and follow up — a model proven in Indian trials like the Healthy Activity Program. Specialists supervise; they don't gatekeep.

Medical Education

More seats, better exposure

Expand psychiatry PG seats and make undergraduate psychiatry postings meaningful — not a two-week formality. Every MBBS graduate should leave able to diagnose depression, start first-line treatment, and know when to refer.

Technology

Telepsychiatry as default, not backup

Strengthen Tele-MANAS with guaranteed staffing, regional languages, and referral pathways that actually end in a bed or a prescription — not just a helpline number read out twice.

Hospitals

Fix the OPD, not the doctor

Appointment slotting, triage by trained nurses, group psychoeducation for stable patients, and separate refill counters — operational changes that return minutes to the consultations that need them.

Society · You

Kill the stigma that delays the visit

The treatment gap isn't only supply — it's silence. Every conversation that treats a psychiatric illness like the medical illness it is shortens someone's path to the clinic by months or years.
